Welcome to the Daughters of Diodati.

We take our name from a rented house above Lake Geneva, an eighteen-year-old girl, and the worst summer anybody could remember.

1816 · The year without a summer

A volcano called Tambora had erupted in Indonesia the year before, throwing up enough ash to dim the sun over an entire hemisphere. Europe simply didn't get a summer. It snowed in June. Harvests failed, bread ran short, and the rain came down for weeks without stopping.

Which is how a small party of English writers ended up shut inside the Villa Diodati, working through a book of German ghost stories by candlelight while the storms came off the water. Bored of reading them, Lord Byron proposed that each of them write one instead.

The girl nobody was watching

In that room sat Mary Godwin — eighteen, unmarried, the only person there who had published nothing. For days she had nothing. Then came the image she said arrived in a waking dream: a young man kneeling beside the thing he had assembled, watching it open its eyes.

She wrote Frankenstein. It was published two years later with no name on it at all, because a novel that good under a woman's name would have been a liability. She was the youngest in the house and she beat every famous man in it.

A group, a bad month, a prompt, a deadline. That's the whole recipe, and it still works.

Why we're doing this

This club is about the female creative mind — and about keeping human thought in human hands.

We are the first generation of writers who can have the work done for us. A machine will hand you competent prose in nine seconds, and it will be fine, and it will be nobody's. What it cannot do is want something. It has never been eighteen and unpublished in a room full of famous men, and it has never had anything to prove.

So we write by hand, in our own voices, on purpose. The strange sentence. The wrong-sounding image. The thing only you would have thought of, because only you have lived where you've lived. Your imagination has been running the whole time, quietly, in the background — this is where we turn the sound up.

Everything read aloud in this room was written by the woman who signs it. That is the one rule with no exceptions.

How the club works

One book, one prompt, six weeks.

Every cycle runs six weeks. We all read the same book, and each of us picks her own prompt from that cycle's set — whichever one grabs you. Every prompt comes with constraints: a point of view, a word limit, and one rule you're not allowed to break. Those limits are the point. They push you somewhere you wouldn't have gone on your own.

  • Weeks one to three

    Read. The prompts go out with the book, so whichever one you choose sits in the back of your mind the whole time you're reading. Three weeks means you can have a bad week and still finish.

  • Week four

    Write. However you work: longhand, notes app, in the car outside your kid's practice. Pieces are due at the end of the week.

  • Week five

    We gather. Everyone has had a few days to read the writing beforehand, so we arrive already knowing each other's pieces. Book first, then the writing. Wine encouraged.

  • Week six

    A breather. The next woman up names her book and posts her prompts, and the whole thing begins again.

Six weeks is the default, not the law.

A four-hundred-page novel is not a hundred-and-seventy-six-page one, and December is not February. Whoever is hosting the cycle sets its length to fit the book she picked and what the rest of us are carrying that month — a long or difficult book buys extra reading weeks, a short one can run tighter.

We work around the calendar the same way. Holidays, the start and end of the school year, exam weeks, travel: if a cycle is going to land badly for most of us, we stretch it or push the meeting rather than let people show up unread and apologetic. The point is that the deadline is real, not that it is rigid.

We rotate who chooses.

Each cycle belongs to a different woman. She picks the book, writes the prompts, sets the constraints, and brings the first question to the table when we meet. Then it passes to the next of us.

That's deliberate. It keeps us out of one genre and one taste, it means nobody is permanently in charge, and it puts every woman here in the position of choosing what a whole room reads and writes for a month — which is its own kind of practice.

Cycle one

The reading

The Wax Child

Olga Ravn, translated from the Danish by Martin Aitken

176 pages · New Directions · Horror

A wax doll tells this story. Christenze Kruckow, an unmarried noblewoman in seventeenth-century Denmark, made it out of beeswax and packed it with the hair and nails of people she disliked — and it has been watching ever since.

What follows is a real witch trial: the whispers, the confessions pulled out of women under torture, the fire at the end of it. Ravn built the voice out of court records, letters, spells and household manuals, so the book moves like an incantation rather than a plot. It's short, strange, and it refuses to resolve.

Fair warning: this one splits rooms. Half of us will find it hypnotic and half will find it airless. That argument is the first meeting.

The prompt

Choose the one that grabs you.

Five to pick from — take whichever one you want. No need to match anyone else, and it's more interesting at the table if we don't. Each prompt steals a technique from the book: an object that narrates, a group that speaks as one, a horror built entirely out of paperwork. Word counts are ceilings, not targets. Come in under them.

The object knows

Narrate a family coming apart from the point of view of something they own. It cannot move, speak, or intervene — it can only notice, and it has opinions.

  • First person
  • Present tense
  • 1,200 words max

We are more than one

A group tells its own story in collective voice — we, never I. In the final paragraph, one member breaks from the we.

  • Collective first person
  • No named narrator
  • 1,000 words max

The evidence file

Tell the whole story through documents: a court transcript, a recipe, a letter never sent, an inventory of a dead woman's belongings. Nothing connects them but the reader.

  • Five documents
  • 250 words each, max
  • No narration

Rumour

Open with the ending — they all burned — then spend the rest of the piece making the reader hope you were lying.

  • Third person
  • No dialogue tags but "said"
  • 800 words max

The spell

Write a set of instructions — a spell, a remedy, a how-to — that slowly gives away something terrible about the person who wrote it.

  • Second person
  • Instructional voice
  • 600 words max
